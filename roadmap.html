mechanics first

It's better to focus on the mechanics and back end first. Make it work, then make it look good, right? Your rule, 25 Aug — the order below follows it

  1. 01added
    7 Sep

    Selecting more than one thing at a timerecommended next

    Everything today acts on exactly one part: the gizmo, delete, the length grips. Selecting several would make delete, move and rotate work on a group — and it is what copy and paste needs underneath it.

    Two things worth deciding before it is built. How you select — drag a box across the grid, or tap parts one after another to add them? A box is quicker for a block; tapping is more precise in a dense layout, and works better with a thumb. Whether a selection can cross layers, or is confined to the one you are working on, the way placement already is.

  2. 02added
    7 Sep

    Copy and pasteneeds 01

    Repeat an assembly you have already worked out instead of rebuilding it cell by cell. For modular pieces this is probably the biggest single saving on the list.

    The real question is where a paste lands: offset a cell from the original, onto the layer you are currently on, or dropped where you next tap. And what happens when it does not fit — refuse the whole paste, or place what fits and say what it left out. Refusing wholesale is safer; partial pasting is friendlier right up until it quietly drops something.

  3. 03added
    31 Aug

    Trace mode

    Follow the flow through a finished layout and see where it actually goes. Your old app has this and the slice has none of it. It sits on top of the existing model rather than changing it, which is why it waits until the editing tools settle.

  4. 04added
    21 Aug

    Valves, and what flow does at a junctionneeds 03

    A valve part, and the logic that goes with it — a merge behaves like an or, a valve like a not or an and. Parked since before the catalogue work, and it needs trace mode underneath it.

  5. 05added

    The whole stack pulled apart along its vertical axis, so you can see every floor at once instead of one at a time. Parked, not dropped.

Just settled

newest first

The isometric reads correctly

settled 19 Sep

Your verdict after the depth-order fix was that it reads well, with two instances left where it did not — both elbows arcing up and away from the camera. They had the same cause. A whole pipe was sorted on the average depth of its length, which is only right for a pipe lying flat to you. On an arc climbing away, the two ends sit at very different depths and the average falls between them, so the pipe was drawn later than its far end deserved and painted over the port sitting there. On a board of 25 elbows, 48 of 100 pipe ends were buried under their own pipe — near enough the half you would predict, since every arc loses at whichever end is farther than its average. Now nought.

Each length of pipe is sorted in short pieces, each with its own depth, and a port is sorted against the pieces that actually reach it — a connector caps the end of a tube, so nothing of that tube can be in front of it.

No depth cue needed after all. The mockup board for breaking one pipe where another crosses it is not required; the order being wrong was the whole problem.

The board can be empty

settled 17 Sep

It could not be before. Three separate things kept at least one part on the board: the delete action refused to remove the last one, a save with no parts was rejected as broken, and the checks panel read the first part without asking whether there was one. All three are lifted, so Clear all parts in the Board menu empties the board and leaves the grid exactly as you set it up — start again on the same frame.

No confirmation, per your standing call that deleting should not ask first. One undo puts the whole board back in a single step, which is what stands in for the prompt. Deleting the last part by hand now works too, for the same reason.

Three faults the owner caught on screen

settled 17 Sep

The 3D view turned about the wrong point. The camera span about the world’s zero point, which stopped being the middle of the board the day the per-end controls let a grid grow lopsided. Grow east, or add layers on top, and the middle walked away from the pivot: 98px out of centre on a 370px stage for a 4×4×8. It now turns about the grid’s own middle, measured at nought drift through three orbits.

The sheet’s isometric was painting back to front. It drew far geometry over near — the opposite of the rule the 3D view and the axis triad already follow — so a part’s near port was buried under its own pipe while its far port sat on top, and the part read as heading away when it came toward you. Long runs were worst because each was sorted by one end only.

Vertical ports were drawn six times the width of sideways ones. Halved, so they read as the same kind of mark. Both now take their colour from the port’s role like everything else — a downward inlet used to draw grey whatever it was.

Parts now fill their cells

settled 17 Sep

Option D from the board: a part is drawn at the proportion the 3D view gives it, so it spans the cells it genuinely occupies. A 3-cell straight measures 2.84 cells where it measured 1.42. Line weight and port size scale with it, so the drawing keeps its weight instead of thinning out.

The isometric panel carried the identical fault — cells spaced twice as far apart as the parts drawn inside them — so it took the same correction and the two panels agree again.

Three corrections to the sheet

settled 16 Sep

Zoom was built wrong and is rebuilt. It kept the page a fixed size and moved a window across it, which felt like peering through a letterbox. The page itself now gets bigger and you push it around behind the opening, the way zooming a drawing should feel. It still cannot be zoomed smaller than a full page, cannot be pushed off into the grey, and print and export are unaffected.

The little isometric in each plan square is gone. It was first cut back to one per part and only where the part ran vertically; on 17 Sep it came out altogether, which was the intent all along. The full isometric panel sits beside the plan on the same sheet, so the small one was saying the same thing twice, and the room it gave back went into drawing the parts at their true size.

A stray dot on flat runs is gone. The section mark meant for a pipe passing vertically through a floor was also landing on the middle cell of a horizontal multi-cell straight, which has no port there either but is already drawn as one line straight through the cell.

How a pipe passing through a floor is drawn

settled 15 Sep

Plan: a port mark where the part has a port on that floor; otherwise a solid dot showing the pipe cut through. The dot is one and a half times the pipe’s width — at exactly the pipe’s width it was faint on every screen — and stays solid ink with no outline, so it cannot be read as a port.

Isometric: the part at its full height on every floor it touches, dimmed on floors where it owns no port.

Outlets are grey on the sheet now, matching the 3D view.

Four decisions closed

settled 7 Sep

The 12×12×12 slowdown stays. Not worth losing the ability to see through the stack for a size you do not work at.

The working layer keeps winning an overlapping tap. Unchanged.

Delete still asks nothing. Undo covers it.

The spec's “40 topologies” was a typo, now corrected to 39 in all three places it appeared. Checked rather than assumed: the document's own per-pattern tallies have always summed to 39 — cap 1, straight 1, elbow 1, T 4, corner 2, cross 4, corner-through 8, five-way 10, six-way 8 — which is exactly what the app builds. No part was ever missing. The spec now carries that table, so its total can no longer drift from its own parts.

And yes, far more variants than the old app. That one had ten genuinely distinct fittings; four of the nine connectivity patterns — corner, corner-through, five-way and six-way — did not exist in it at all. Its three riser entries were vertical straights of one, two and three cells, which is precisely what a multi-cell straight replaced: three catalogue entries collapsed into one part with a length.

A six-way split is not a six-way merge

settled 5 Sep

I'd assumed the 39 catalogue entries collapsed to 9 physical fittings, since they share port layouts and differ only in which openings are inlets. You corrected that: they're cast stoneware, and the connectors are what align each piece for gluing, so the roles are the object.

So the list was already grouping correctly. What it was missing is the thing that identifies which mould to pull — now on every row, in the part's own orientation, so a rotated fitting still counts as the same fitting:

six-way, single-anchor split · in N · out S E W U D six-way, single-anchor merge · in S E W U D · out N

Multi-cell parts landed

settled 6 Sep

A cast piece can now be one, two or three cells. Length is part of what the object is, so it saves with the board and gives each size its own line in the cut list.

Both ends of a run carry a grip; pulling one leaves the other where it is. Drag past three cells and the run divides itself into 3s and 2s — the same rule your old app used, and also the fewest pieces possible for any length. The drag and the dividing are one gesture rather than two features: a cast stops at three, so without the dividing a drag would simply hit a wall.

A riser already works as a run pointing upward, and since 10 Sep it is drawn on every floor it crosses.

Settled earlier

settled 5 Sep

PDF stays as it is. Export to PNG is one tap; a PDF goes through Safari’s own dialog. Not worth an outside library in a file that otherwise needs nothing to run.

Multi-cell comes before multi-layer. Your call, and the right one — a riser is just a multi-cell part standing up, so the general case belongs first.

Roboto Slab throughout, and the sheet’s toolbar rebuilt: a segmented layer control sharing its labels with the 3D view, PNG as the primary action, print secondary.
