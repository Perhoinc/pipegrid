Waiting on you

5 open

None of these block the build — I can keep going around all of them. But each one is a call I shouldn't make on your behalf.

The largest grid is still slow. Fix it, or leave it?

A 12×12×12 grid takes 13.8 milliseconds to draw each frame, against 1.5 for your 4×4×8. That's 1,727 cells of genuine geometry — there's no waste left to remove.

The only real fix is to draw just the outside shell of the stack and hide the interior. Faster, but you'd stop seeing through it.

My read Leave it until you actually work at that size. If you get there, we decide then — it's a change to what you see, not just how fast it is.

When layers overlap on screen, which one takes the tap?

Seen at an angle, every layer sits on top of every other. A part one floor up routinely covers an empty cell you're aiming at — close enough to steal the tap.

I made the layer you're working on win. So to select a part on another layer, you switch to that layer first. The alternative is that parts always win, and you orbit slightly to reach a blocked cell.

My read Keep it as is — but you're the one whose thumb knows. Worth a few minutes on the iPad before you decide.

Should deleting ask first?

Right now it doesn't. Delete removes the selected part immediately, and Undo puts it straight back.

My read No confirmation. A dialog on every removal costs more than the occasional mis-tap it prevents, and Undo already covers it.

How should the pipes themselves look in 3D?

They're still flat centre-lines. None of the mark grammar from the old app — pipe-colouring for structure, shadowing under rings, chevrons for shared bores — has reached the 3D view.

Two directions: carry that 2D grammar across as-is, or design something properly three-dimensional — shading, silhouette, real pipe thickness.

My read A 3D equivalent, and on a mockup board first, per your own rule. But this sits behind the mechanics work below either way.

The shape picker glyphs

You flagged these as not great when you picked the dock layout, and they haven't been touched since. There's one simple icon per connectivity family rather than per variant, so a six-way split and a six-way merge look identical in the picker.

My read Now that we know the port configuration is what identifies a part, the glyphs arguably need to show inlets and outlets — which makes this a bigger job than redrawing icons.

The parts spec counts 40 topologies. Its own tally adds to 39.

I built 39 and flagged this at the time. Every entry the document itself lists is accounted for, so it's most likely a typo in the prose rather than a missing part — but it has never been settled.

My read Almost certainly a doc error. Worth ten minutes of your eyes on the spec to close it, since it's the authority we build the catalogue from.

Next

mechanics first

It's better to focus on the mechanics and back end first. Make it work, then make it look good, right? Your rule, 25 Aug — the order below follows it

  1. 01

    Risers, and drawing a part that crosses floorsrecommended next

    The mechanics already work — a run pointing upward is a riser, and that arrived with the multi-cell work. What is left is the drawing: showing a part on every floor it passes through. That part is designed already — full height shown, the current floor solid, the rest ghosted, and no port at all on the middle floors, since a port there would claim a connection that does not exist on that floor.

  2. 02

    Selecting more than one thing at a time

    Everything today acts on exactly one part: the gizmo, delete, the length grips. Selecting several would make delete, move and rotate work on a group — and it is what copy and paste needs underneath it.

    Two things worth deciding before it is built. How you select — drag a box across the grid, or tap parts one after another to add them? A box is quicker for a block; tapping is more precise in a dense layout, and works better with a thumb. Whether a selection can cross layers, or is confined to the one you are working on, the way placement already is.

  3. 03

    Copy and pasteneeds 02

    Repeat an assembly you have already worked out instead of rebuilding it cell by cell. For modular pieces this is probably the biggest single saving on the list.

    The real question is where a paste lands: offset a cell from the original, onto the layer you are currently on, or dropped where you next tap. And what happens when it does not fit — refuse the whole paste, or place what fits and say what it left out. Refusing wholesale is safer; partial pasting is friendlier right up until it quietly drops something.

  4. 04

    Trace mode

    Follow the flow through a finished layout and see where it actually goes. Your old app has this and the slice has none of it. It sits on top of the existing model rather than changing it, which is why it waits until the editing tools settle.

  5. 05

    Valves, and what flow does at a junctionneeds 04

    A valve part, and the logic that goes with it — a merge behaves like an or, a valve like a not or an and. Parked since before the catalogue work, and it needs trace mode underneath it.

  6. 06

    Exploded stack viewparked

    The whole stack pulled apart along its vertical axis, so you can see every floor at once instead of one at a time. Parked, not dropped.

  7. 07

    Pipe treatment in 3Dafter mechanics

    See the open question above. Deliberately behind the mechanics work, per your own sequencing rule.

Just settled

the cut list

A six-way split is not a six-way merge

I'd assumed the 39 catalogue entries collapsed to 9 physical fittings, since they share port layouts and differ only in which openings are inlets. You corrected that: they're cast stoneware, and the connectors are what align each piece for gluing, so the roles are the object.

So the list was already grouping correctly. What it was missing is the thing that identifies which mould to pull — now on every row, in the part's own orientation, so a rotated fitting still counts as the same fitting:

six-way, single-anchor split · in N · out S E W U D six-way, single-anchor merge · in S E W U D · out N

Multi-cell parts landed

A cast piece can now be one, two or three cells. Length is part of what the object is, so it saves with the board and gives each size its own line in the cut list.

Both ends of a run carry a grip; pulling one leaves the other where it is. Drag past three cells and the run divides itself into 3s and 2s — the same rule your old app used, and also the fewest pieces possible for any length. The drag and the dividing are one gesture rather than two features: a cast stops at three, so without the dividing a drag would simply hit a wall.

A riser already works as a run pointing upward. Only the drawing side is left, which is item 01.

Settled earlier

PDF stays as it is. Export to PNG is one tap; a PDF goes through Safari’s own dialog. Not worth an outside library in a file that otherwise needs nothing to run.

Multi-cell comes before multi-layer. Your call, and the right one — a riser is just a multi-cell part standing up, so the general case belongs first.

Roboto Slab throughout, and the sheet’s toolbar rebuilt: a segmented layer control sharing its labels with the 3D view, PNG as the primary action, print secondary.
