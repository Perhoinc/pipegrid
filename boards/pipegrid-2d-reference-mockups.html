one layer of the stack, for the bench · three ways of showing which way a part faces
THE PROBLEM, IN ONE LINE
A flat plan draws N/E/S/W ports as channels without trouble. U and D ports have nowhere to go — they collapse onto the cell's centre. v38 solved that for a handful of vertical poses with the ⊙ / under-ring grammar; the question is whether that survives parts which can now face any of 24 ways. The same layer is drawn three ways below. Every plan shows the identical parts — only the treatment of vertical ports differs.
⊙ opening faces UP
◍ opening dives DOWN (under-ring)
▲▼ shared vertical bore, continues to next layer
amber = inlet
TREATMENTS — SAME LAYER, THREE WAYS
A · v38 grammar, extended
Channels in plane, vertical ports as centre glyphs. Nothing new to learn if you already read v38 drawings. Watch the busy cells — where several marks land on one centre, does it still read?
B · plan + iso thumbnail
Same plan, but each cell also carries a small 3D thumbnail of the actual posed part. Unambiguous — you see the real orientation — at the cost of a busier drawing and small marks at bench distance.
C · plan + edge ticks
Channels in plane, plus a tick on the cell edge for every port and a corner badge reading U / D / UD for vertical ones. Reads like a dimensioned drawing rather than a picture.
CUT LIST — does orientation belong here?
Your hard law says a rotated fitting is the same fitting, so poses never multiply the bill. v38 still breaks the list down by orientation, which was useful when there were four poses. With 24 it may just be noise — and a corner-split is one object to fabricate however it ends up sitting.
Parts only — what you fabricate
Short. Every line is a thing you actually make. Orientation lives on the plan, where assembly happens.
Broken down by orientation — v38 style
Every distinct orientation gets its own line. Truthful, but it's assembly information wearing a cut list's clothes, and it grows fast.
My read: the plain list is the cut list, and orientation belongs on the plan beside the part it describes — the sheet you carry to the bench already shows you which way it goes. But you're the one who'll be holding both.
WHAT I STILL DON'T KNOW
  • Does one layer at a time actually work for a part that spans layers — a riser, or anything whose bore continues upward? It appears on two sheets. The ▲▼ chevrons say "this continues", but you may want the neighbouring layer ghosted in as well.
  • How much does the plan need to say about flow? Right now inlets are amber. For fabrication you may not care about flow at all — it's a welding drawing, not a schematic — or it may be exactly what stops you assembling something backwards.
  • Sheet identity. Nothing here names the layer, the job, or the date. A reference you carry to a bench probably wants a title block.
